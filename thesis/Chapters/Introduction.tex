In the coming years, significant changes are expected to occur within the realm of the Internet-of-Things (IoT) because of new and improved technologies in countless sectors. It is estimated that around 500 billion devices will be connected to the Internet by the year 2030 \cite{FutureNetworks2030}, and that almost all kinds of different electrical systems will eventually become part of IoT. With the emergence of the Edge/Fog Computing paradigm, it is likely that the enormous amount of data produced by sensors and other IoT devices will be processed far away from big hyperscale data centers, thus spanning a dense network of connected edge devices around the globe \cite{EdgeCompVision}.

Over time, the power consumption and therefore the carbon footprint of such a distributed network will become an issue if solely powered by the public grid, as most of the energy in today's world is produced using fossil fuels \cite{SustainableEdgeComputing}. However, in the same way that the computing power is projected to become more spread out, the utilization of distributed renewable energy resources based on less-powerful generators appears to be a promising alternative to meet the power demand \cite{MicrogridReview}.

The Microgrid concept provides a fitting framework for defining the operation of such distributed generators and consumers \cite{Microgrid}, and recent work has outlined the advantages of Microgrids over traditional energy systems in terms of control, flexibility, economic viability, environmental friendliness, and adaptability \cite{MicrogridPerspectives}. Because of the rather volatile nature of renewable energy sources like solar and wind, the energy output of a localized small-sized Microgrid will fluctuate over time, which makes the use of energy storage systems such as batteries necessary to deal with upcoming imbalances between demand and supply side within this kind of environment \cite{MicrogridStorageAdvances}.

To study load and battery management as well as other dynamic behaviors of a microgrid, new models have to be built, treating it as a system of systems because at its core, a microgrid comprises a complex interplay of various components, including renewable energy sources, energy storage systems, conventional generators, power electronics, and intelligent control algorithms \cite{alzahrani2017modeling}. Co-simulation offers a platform to study such coupled heterogeneous systems by composing simulations of its different parts \cite{gomes2018co}, and a tool called Vessim \cite{wiesner2023testbed} has already been proposed recently, utilizing this approach to serve as a virtual testbed.
