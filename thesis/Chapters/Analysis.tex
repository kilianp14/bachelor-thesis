In this chapter, the implementation of four models for lithium-ion batteries is discussed, and their behavior in a co-simulation environment, depicting a data center's power system, is analyzed. Thereby, Section \ref{battery_models_for_co_simulation} introduces and parameterizes the used battery models, and Section \ref{experimental_results_and_discussion} discusses the experimental results of different scenarios.

\section{Battery Models for Co-Simulation}
\label{battery_models_for_co_simulation}
The explained battery models are parameterized to represent a battery pack of rechargeable Lithium-Ion INR21700 M50 cells \footnote{The product specification can be found under this link: \url{https://www.dnkpower.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/LG-INR21700-M50-Datasheet.pdf}}. For experimentation, a pack of 16 cells is used, whereas four series groups are connected in parallel (4S4P) as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:pack_circuit}.

There are four different mathematical models described in total that increase in complexity. Section \ref{simple_battery} discusses the simplest lossless model, Section \ref{clc_battery} adds power constraints and inefficiencies to the model, in Section \ref{pybamm_battery} electrochemistry of individual cells are considered, and Section \ref{liion_battery_pack} extends this to include losses due to the battery pack's circuit on top.

\subsection{SimpleBattery}
\label{simple_battery}
The \textbf{SimpleBattery} only receives two parameters upon initialization, the battery's energy capacity $C$ in $Wh$ and the initial SoC, which is used for determining the battery's initial energy $b(0)$. When updated, given the power $p_s(t)$ and the duration $d_s(t)$ of that time-step $t$, the battery's new energy is then computed using this formula:
\begin{equation}
b(t) = \begin{dcases}
    \min(C,\: b(t-1) + p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t)) & \vert\: p_s(t) \ge 0\\
    \max(0,\: b(t-1) + p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t)) & \vert\: p_s(t) < 0\\
\end{dcases}
\end{equation}

The (dis-)charged energy that is to be returned when implementing the proposed storage interface, is $p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t)$ in the default case, $-b(t-1)$ if the battery is fully discharged, or $C - b(t-1)$ if the battery is fully charged during the time-step. The battery's state of charge can be simply determined as $\text{SoC}(t) = \frac{b(t)}{C}$.

The parameterization of this model to reflect the mentioned battery pack only requires a value for the battery capacity $C$. To ensure comparability between the different models, it was determined using a PyBaMM simulation of a lithium-ion cell with the SPMe model and the parameterization of the used cell by Chen et al. \cite{chen2020development}. A complete discharge at $0.2C$ of a single cell returned an energy of $C = 18.87Wh$ compared to the $18.20Wh$ that is listed in the product specification. This value is just multiplied by the number of cells to get the total energy capacity of a battery pack.

\subsection{CLCBattery}
\label{clc_battery}
The \textbf{CLCBattery} implements the C-L-C model by Kazhamiaka et al. \cite{kazhamiaka2019tractable}, which has been presented in detail in Section \ref{c_l_c_model}. When updated, the model behaves similar to the \textbf{SimpleBattery}, but includes constant inefficiency factors, so that the updated energy level can be computed based on the Equations \ref{eq:2.1} and \ref{eq:2.2}:
\begin{equation}
    b(t) = \begin{dcases}
    b(t-1) + \eta_c \cdot p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t) & \vert\: p_s(t) \ge 0\\
    b(t-1) + \eta_d \cdot p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t) & \vert\: p_s(t) < 0\\
\end{dcases}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{thesis/Images/charging_efficiency.pdf}
    \caption{(Dis-)charging efficiencies based on battery voltage and (dis-)charging currents, as determined using PyBaMM.}
    \label{fig:charging_efficiency}
\end{figure}

The \textbf{CLCBattery} adds a lot of complexity regarding battery limitations, though. With a constant voltage $V$, as assumed in the model, and linear energy limits $a_1(I(t)) = u_1 \cdot I(t) + v_1$ and $a_2(I(t)) = u_2 \cdot I(t) + v_2$, the Equations \ref{eq:2.3}, \ref{eq:2.4} and \ref{eq:2.5} result in the following power limitations when charging or discharging at each time-step $t$:
\begin{align}
    \alpha_d \cdot V &\le p_s(t) \le \alpha_c \cdot V \\
    \frac{b(t-1) - v_1}{\frac{u_1}{V} - d_s(t) \cdot \eta_d} &\le p_s(t) \le \frac{b(t-1) - v_2}{\frac{u_2}{V} - d_s(t) \cdot \eta_c}
\end{align}
These limitations can also just be scaled linearly by the number of lithium-ion cells when considering battery packs.

Whenever the input power $p_s(t)$ would these (dis-)charging limits, the BMS simply continues the (dis-)charge at the maximum allowed power. The total energy stored or discharged to be returned is therefore given by $e(t) = p_s(t) \cdot d_s(t)$. Similar to the \textbf{SimpleBattery}, the SoC can be easily computed as $\text{SoC}(t) = \frac{b(t)}{v_2}$.

In the C-L-C model, the voltage $V(t)$ is assumed to be constant, and for the experiments set to the nominal voltage of the modeled lithium-ion cell, which is $3.63V$ according to the product specification. The (dis-)charging current limits are defined to be $\alpha_d = -1.5C$ and $\alpha_c = 0.7C$ at $25^{\circ}C$ in the same way.

The constant inefficiency factors $\eta_c$ and $\eta_d$ were determined again using PyBaMM simulations as shown in Figure \ref{fig:charging_efficiency}. The charging efficiency is thereby shown based on the voltage $V$ and the charging rate, whereas the simulation computed the internal battery resistance $R_i$ used to evaluate the formula described by Kazhamiaka et al. \cite{kazhamiaka2019tractable}:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_c(I, V) = 1 - \frac{I \cdot R_i}{V}
\end{equation}
The approximations for the inefficiencies are the values of these functions at the standard discharge rate of $0.2C$, and the standard charge rate of $0.3C$ at the nominal voltage of $3.63V$. The resulting inefficiencies for the cell described by the \textbf{CLCBattery} are therefore fixed at $\eta_d = 1.014$, and $\eta_c = 0.978$.

    \caption{Energy limits of a single battery cell based on the (dis-)charging current.}

Finally, the linear approximations of the upper and lower energy limits, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:energy_limits}, were again determined by PyBaMM simulations:
$$a_1(I) = -0.087 \cdot I \text{,}\:\:\:\: a_2(I) = -1.326 \cdot I + 19.14$$
For battery packs, these energy limits can be scaled linearly by the number of individual cells as well.

\subsection{PybammBattery}
\label{pybamm_battery}
The \textbf{PybammBattery} uses the PyBaMM framework \cite{pybammbatteries} directly to simulate an individual lithium-ion cell. For the experiments, the Single Particle Model with electrolyte, which was described in Section \ref{single_particle_models}, is used together with an external circuit submodel to enable power-based inputs instead of current-based inputs. The single cell model is used with the parameterization for the Lithium-Ion INR21700 M50 cell as experimentally obtained by Chen et al. \cite{chen2020development}. To simulate battery packs, the input power $p_s(t)$ of time-step $t$ is divided by the number of cells before the PyBaMM simulation is stepped. 

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{thesis/Images/soc_estimation.pdf}
    \caption{Relation between the battery cell's State-of-Charge and Open-Circuit Voltage as obtained using PyBaMM.}
    \label{fig:soc_estimation}
\end{figure}

To ensure that limits of the battery cells are not exceeded, and visibility into the state is provided, the model is wrapped with BMS functionality. Firstly, the SoC is determined using the battery's Open-Circuit Voltage (OCV), which is the battery's difference in potential when resting, together with an OCV–SoC lookup table, as visualized in Figure \ref{fig:soc_estimation}. This only provides a fairly accurate estimation of the SoC because the exact OCV is known in the model. In a real scenario, the method could not be used, as the OCV cannot be measured accurately. Additionally, the relationship between the OCV and the SoC changes with temperature and battery age \cite{meng2017overview}.

Secondly, also based on the OCV, power limits were determined to ensure that the battery does not exceed allowed currents and voltages when being (dis-)charged with a certain power. In particular, the charging rates cannot exceed $1.5C$, equivalent to $7.5A$, for discharging, and $0.7C$, equivalent to $3.5A$, for charging, whereas the battery cell's voltage is supposed to stay between $2.5V$ and $4.2V$. The power limits depending on the OCV were determined using two PyBaMM simulations and are shown in Figure \ref{fig:power_limits}. When the input power $p_s(t)$ would exceed the power limits based on the state, the maximum applicable power is used for (dis-)charging and computation of the stored or discharged energy, which has to be returned.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{thesis/Images/power_limits.pdf}
    \caption{Power limits for charging and discharging based on the cell's OCV. In the more linear parts, the limits are the result of current boundaries, whereas voltage boundaries cause power limitations at a low SoC when discharging and at high SoC when charging.}
    \label{fig:power_limits}
\end{figure}

\subsection{LiionBatteryPack}
\label{liion_battery_pack}
The \textbf{LiionBatteryPack} utilizes PyBaMM \cite{pybammbatteries} for simulation of battery cells, as well as methods for computing power-losses and solving power networks by Liionpack \cite{tranter2022liionpack} to model an entire lithium-ion battery pack, such as the one visualized in Figure \ref{fig:pack_circuit}.Thereby, a series of steps are performed to determine the new battery state at every time-step $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The internal resistances and voltages are computed for every individual cell and included in the network representation.
    \item The network is solved based on the input power $p_s(t)$, which determines the applied currents for the cells.
    \item The electrochemical system of each cell is stepped with their respective current input using PyBaMM.
\end{enumerate}

Again, this model is wrapped with a BMS. The SoC is computed for all the cells in the same way as for the \textbf{PybammBattery}, and then averaged in the end. The power limits are also determined similar to the PyBaMM model described in Section \ref{pybamm_battery}, as the power limit is computed for the cell with the highest voltage when charging or the cell with the lowest voltage when discharging, and then scaled by the number of batteries in the battery pack. The determined applied power is once again used to compute the returned (dis-)charged energy by multiplication with the duration $d_s(t)$.
