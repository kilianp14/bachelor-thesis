\section{Microgrids}
\label{microgrids}
Economic, technological, and environmental factors are driving changes in how electricity is generated and transmitted. The dominance of large centralized power plants is diminishing and giving way to smaller, more decentralized models, partly due to the decreasing advantages of traditional economies of scale \cite{MicrogridConcept}. The concept of a microgrid was first presented by Lasseter \cite{Microgrid} as early as 2002, describing a paradigm to define the operation of distributed generation. The core idea is to perceive such generators and associated loads as a system of its own to allow for local control, thereby providing a higher local reliability and efficiency.

The CIGRÉ Working Group C6.22 \textit{Microgrid Evolution Roadmap}  defines the following components as the foundational elements of a microgrid \cite{MicrogridKeyComponents}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[] \textbf{Generators} encompass various energy sources suitable for microgrids, including fossil or biomass-fired small-scale combined heat and power, photovoltaic modules, wind turbines, etc.
    \item[] \textbf{Storage Devices} include diverse technologies such as electrical, electrochemical, mechanical, and heat storage.
    \item[] \textbf{Controlled loads} refer to all kinds of electrical loads within a microgrid that can be managed or controlled to optimize energy usage and system performance.
\end{itemize}

In addition to that, a microgrid can operate in two different modes: grid-connected and islanded \cite{MicrogridConcept, MicrogridKeyComponents, MicrogridComprehensiveStudy}. In the grid-connected mode, the microgrid functions as a part of the larger utility grid, while drawing power from the grid as needed, enabling it to benefit from the main grids stability and reliability. During certain phases like grid outages, the microgrid is capable of a seamless transition into islanded mode where it disconnects from the grid and operates autonomously, relying solely on its own distributed energy resources to meet local energy demands. Effective control and coordination mechanisms are thereby essential for smooth transitions between these two modes, to optimize energy management and ensure reliable operation under varying conditions.

\section{Co-Simulation}
\label{co-simulation}
The purpose of simulations is to recreate a real-world system or scenario in a controlled environment, and it is a powerful tool in that regard. They eliminate the risks and costs associated with doing certain experimentation on a real system or hardware while testing relevant hypotheses, and thereby allow insights into complex scenarios by predicting real-world outcomes. In a world of ever-increasing system complexity, co-simulation presents a way to leverage from established tools for the simulation of subsystems, by allowing the concurrent execution of different simulators \cite{schweiger2019empirical}. In that way, more complex systems can be built by simply defining the interactions between them.

Co-simulation encompasses the theoretical framework as well as practical methodologies, involving simulation of interconnected systems through the integration of multiple simulators. In this approach, each simulator serves as a self-contained entity, analogous to a black box, possessing the capability to emulate specific behaviors, consume inputs, and generate outputs \cite{gomes2018co}. Therefore, the concept of co-simulation does not attempt to fit an entire system into a single monolithic simulation environment, but instead separates diverse subsystems, which have their distinct computational models or, in some cases, different physical laws.

In a co-simulated scenario, each individual component or subsystem has its simulation unit that produces a behavior trace (or simulation) when given inputs, and, if it is not a real-world entity, has the following components: A Model of the system under study that is a dynamical system with a state and a notion of evolution rules, and a Solver/Simulator that works as an algorithm computing or approximating the behavior trace of a model \cite{gomes2018co, schweiger2019empirical}. When these simulation units are coupled using their inputs and outputs, they produce a combined behavior trace of the entire modeled system, which is called co-simulation.

Because the simulation units are treated as independent black-boxes, to run a co-simulation, a separate orchestrator is needed, next to the co-simulation scenario, to handle the coupling at the behavioral level. The orchestrator is responsible for the initialization of the simulation units, the connection of their inputs and outputs, and the progression of simulated time in the individual components. Gomes et al. \cite{gomes2018co} defines three different approaches for the operation of orchestrators:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[] In \textbf{Discrete-Event} based co-simulation, the scenario consists of multiple discrete-event simulation units, which means that they communicate with the environment using timestamped events. Their internal state can change despite the simulation time being stopped (reactivity), and events can trigger other events instantaneously (transiency). The orchestrator hereby handles the mapping between output events of one unit to external events in another unit.
    \item[] In \textbf{Continuous-Time} based co-simulation, the state of each continuous-time simulation unit evolves constantly. The orchestrator has to set a communication step-size, defining the times when the units exchange their input/output values because the simulation units each have independent micro step-sizes.
    \item[] Finally, in \textbf{Hybrid} co-simulation, the characteristics of both kinds of simulation units are combined. The orchestrator can be either discrete-event based or continuous-time based, whereas the behavior of the simulation units have to be wrapped to become discrete-event or continuous-time based units.
\end{itemize}

Within a co-simulation environment, each simulation unit is operated as an independent module using its individual simulation algorithm. By selecting and implementing appropriate models for each subsystem, this modularity ensures that the models are more specialized and tailored to the characteristics of its modeled subsystem. With the described black-box approach, co-simulation treats individual components as interchangeable, which means that as long as the inputs and outputs of a component adhere to a predefined interface, they can be swapped with alternative components without making extensive modifications to the general system architecture necessary. Therefore, co-simulation allows for more flexibility compared to a monolithic approach.

Following this, the next sections will present relevant frameworks for this work using co-simulation, where Section \ref{mosaik_framework} explains the Mosaik framework \cite{mosaik}, and Section \ref{vessim_infrastructure} introduces Vessim \cite{vessim}, which is used for evaluating the approaches of this thesis and builds on top of Mosaik.

\subsection{The Mosaik Framework}
\label{mosaik_framework}
Mosaik \cite{mosaik} was designed to be used as a co-simulation framework to study scenarios in the area of the smart grid \cite{Smart-Grids}, which can, for instance, serve as testbeds for different control strategies in said smart grid. Nowadays, however, it can easily be used as a flexible co-simulator for various environments by combining different simulation units to create large-scale scenarios.

At its core, Mosaik is responsible for synchronizing processes that each run a simulation, and to manage data-flows between them. To achieve this, the framework encompasses four main components \footnote{These main components are listed in the Mosaik documentation under the following link: \url{https://mosaik.readthedocs.io/en/3.3.0/overview.html}}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[] \textbf{Mosaik API:} Establishes a common protocol for communication between the Mosaik-core and the coupled simulators. While the low-level API is based on network sockets for the passing of JSON messages, there are also high-level APIs available for various programming languages, which provide an extra abstraction layer by handling the networking.
    \item[] \textbf{Scenario API:} Python API for creating simulation scenarios, which makes it possible to define data flows by connecting simulation units, and to execute these scenarios by starting the different simulation units. 
    \item[] \textbf{Simulation Manager:} Handles external simulator processes and communicates with them. They are internally represented by proxy objects so that it makes no difference to other Mosaik components if the simulators are in-process with the Mosaik-core, started by Mosaik itself, or if Mosaik simply connected to a running simulator instance.
    \item[] \textbf{Mosaik Scheduler:} The discrete-event orchestrator of the co-simulation. It is responsible for stepping the simulators through time while keeping them synchronized, and the data passing of inputs and outputs between the simulation units. Mosaik allows these simulation units to be continuous-time based or hybrid by wrapping them in discrete-event simulation units.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Vessim Infrastructure}
\label{vessim_infrastructure}
With the goal of accelerating research in the area of sustainable computing, Wiesner et al. recently presented the Vessim Framework \cite{vessim}, which evolved in the last year to provide a co-simulation test environment for the development of carbon-aware applications and systems that interact with their power system. It allows users to define individual microgrids to represent these power systems of computing infrastructure like data centers, utilizing the described Mosaik framework under the hood. Thereby, each microgrid consists of multiple simulation units responsible for modeling generators, consumers, and energy storage as well as user-defined controllers, which can provide applications visibility and control over the power system.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{thesis/Images/Vessim_Infrastructure.pdf}
    \caption{Microgrid co-simulation infrastructure as described by Wiesner et al. \cite{vessim}. Colored components mark Mosaik simulation units.}
    \label{fig:vessim_infrastructure}
\end{figure*}

The co-simulation infrastructure of a single microgrid, which is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:vessim_infrastructure}, is defined using the Mosaik Scenario API, and the individual simulation units implement the high-level Python API of Mosaik. A Vessim microgrid consists of four distinct subsystems that are stepped in a specific order, whereas the Mosaik Scheduler handles data passing and synchronization.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Actors:} The actors model the energy consumers and producers of the microgrid, and are characterized by their individual power trace $p^{<\text{actor}>}$. Every actor has its separate Mosaik simulation unit, which forwards the actor's power production ($p_t^{<\text{actor}>} > 0$) or power consumption ($p_t^{<\text{actor}>} < 0$) at every simulation step $t$. The actor models are highly variable as they can be everything from simulated nodes, which only read out historical traces of, for instance, solar irradiance or computation load, to nodes that monitor full on server farms, running hundreds of virtual machines where the power consumption is measured using power meters. Due to this high diversity, the actors also have the option to provide controllers with additional information to allow for better decision-making by transmitting their state ($\text{state}_t^{<\text{actor}>}$) to them.
    \item  \textbf{Grid:} The Grid simulator is responsible to model the power flow inside the microgrid's power network. At every simulation step $t$, it gets the power trace of the actors as input, and produces a power delta $\Delta p_t$ that can be stored to or has to be discharged from the energy storage. To accurately model this power flow, frameworks like PyPSA \cite{brown2017pypsa} can be used, but for many cases, a simple aggregation $\Delta p_t = \sum{p_t^{<\text{actor}>}}$ is enough.
    \item \textbf{Controllers:} Users can define custom controllers that can monitor and configure the power and computing systems during the simulation. Controllers receive the entire state of the microgrid's power system at every simulation step $t$. Next to the state of all the actors ($\text{state}_t^{<\text{actor}>}$) and the state of the energy storage ($\text{state}_t^{s}$), this also includes the current power delta ($\Delta p_t$) as well as the excess energy or energy deficit that the power system had since the last time step ($e_{t-1}$). With all this information, which can also be extended by other signals like production and load forecasts, the controllers have the flexibility, next to monitoring, to also reduce consumption by throttling the computing system or tweak the energy storage configuration.
    \item \textbf{Storage:} The storage simulator manages the charging and discharging of the microgrid's energy storage (if present), and the exchanging of energy with the public utility grid. With that, it essentially models the progression of time between simulation time steps while the other subsystems are effectively executed simultaneously at the time steps. It gets the computed ($\Delta p_t$), and provides information regarding the new state of the storage ($\text{state}^s_{t+1}$), and the amount of energy that has been drawn from/ fed into the public grid between the time steps to the controllers ($e_t$). Because the storage simulator is stepped after the controllers, the controllers always receive the information from the previous time step.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis aims to extend upon the Vessim approach by further specifying the storage subsystem of the discussed simulated microgrid. This refined infrastructure will then be used together with simple and complex battery models to show that the described environment can be used as a co-simulation testbed for data centers that allows for the use of variable energy storage modeling approaches.

\section{Battery Management Systems}
\label{battery_management_systems}
A Battery Management System (BMS) is a key part of an energy storage system because, in the context of a microgrid, it is responsible to monitor and control the energy storage functions. Thereby, the central goal of using a BMS is to operate the corresponding battery in an efficient, safe, and reliable manner \cite{rahimi2013battery, bergveld2002battery}.

To avoid unsafe conditions, a BMS can, for instance, prevent the battery from deep discharging, overcharging in an already full state, or exceeding limits like voltage cutoffs, charging or discharging currents typically defined by a charging rate (C-rate), or internal battery temperature \cite{rahimi2013battery}. All that could cause damage to the battery's structure, and thus, the BMS serves as a safety net in that regard.

For various systems, it is also necessary to estimate a battery's state, which can be achieved using a BMS. Some of the most important metrics are the State-of-Charge (SoC), and the State-of-Health (SoH). The SoC describes the available energy still present in the storage system and is typically presented as a percentage of its total energy capacity, whereas the SoH is defined as the maximum possible battery charge compared to the battery's capacity, and informs on battery aging. Note that both of these metrics cannot be measured directly, but have to be estimated in a real-world scenario \cite{rahimi2013battery}.

\section{Lithium-Ion Battery Models}
\label{lithium_ion_battery_models}
The main use cases of lithium-ion batteries (or li-ion batteries) are electric and hybrid vehicles as well as portable electronic devices, due to properties such as their energy density, long cycle life, and low self-discharge rate \cite{kim2019lithium, deng2015li}. The main components of those batteries are the positive electrode (Cathode), the negative electrode (Anode), an electrolyte fluid, and a separator, preventing physical contact between the electrodes. A simplified structure of a lithium-ion battery is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:lithium_ion_battery}.

When an external voltage is applied to the battery during charging, it forces lithium ions from the cathode, which contains a lithium metal oxide, to move through the electrolyte to the anode, where it is inserted into graphite layers \cite{deng2015li}. The electrons released during the electrochemical reaction cannot pass through the separator, and instead flow through the external circuit to balance the charge after being collected by a metal sheet.

During discharging, the opposite process occurs, as electrons move through the external circuit to the cathode, inducing a current to be used by the load, while the lithium-ions pass the electrolyte to become once again part of the lithium-metal-oxide \cite{deng2015li}. Because the difference in electrical charge decreases over time, the terminal voltage of the battery falls as it empties.

The next subsections present a quick overview of relevant models of lithium-ion batteries that are applied in this thesis, whereas battery modeling describes the process of creating mathematical representations to predict the transformation of the battery state under varying conditions such as charging, discharging and resting. Section \ref{c_l_c_model} discusses the C/L/C model as described by Kazhamiaka et al. \cite{kazhamiaka2019tractable}, and Section \ref{single_particle_models} presents single particle models for lithium-ion batteries.

\subsection{C/L/C Model}
\label{c_l_c_model}
The C/L/C is a tractable implementation of the Power-based Integrated (PI) Model as proposed by Kazhamiaka et al. in 2018 \cite{kazhamiaka2018pimodel}, which tries to estimate the energy content of a lithium-ion battery cell. In the PI model, the battery consists of a Battery Management System (BMS) as well as lithium-ion cells, and the change of the battery system's energy level is modeled while the storage is being charged or discharged at varying power over time.

In every time-step $t$, the PI model gets the applied power $p(t)$, the energy content of the previous time-step $b(t-1)$, as well as the duration of the time-step $d$ as inputs to compute the battery's energy $b(t)$. This is achieved by adding a specific energy delta (as shown in equation \ref{eq:2.1}), which depends on all these inputs \footnote{The equations for the PI model in this chapter are taken from the paper by Kazhamiaka et al. \cite{kazhamiaka2018pimodel}.}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.1}
    b(t) = b(t-1) + \Delta_E(t)
\end{equation}

The mentioned energy delta of a step $t$ is computed by multiplying the applied power $p(t)$\footnote{$p(t) > 0$ indicates charging and $p(t) < 0$ indicates discharging.} times the duration $d$ and a (dis-)charging penalty, marking the power loss due to inefficiencies (see equation \ref{eq:2.2}). Thereby, $\eta_c(I, V)$ (charging inefficiency) describes a function representing the fraction of power stored in the battery while it is charged with a certain power $p$, while $\eta_d(I, V)$ (discharging inefficiency) stands for the function representing the fraction of power that has to be discharged from the battery to obtain the power $p$. Subsequently, $0 \le \eta_c(I, V) \le 1$ and $\eta_c(I, V) \ge 1$ hold true. Note that the charging and discharging inefficiencies are dependent on the battery's current $I(t)$, and the battery's voltage $V(t)$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.2}
    \Delta_E(k) = \begin{dcases} \eta_c(I(t),V(t))\cdot p(t)\cdot d & \vert\: p(t) \ge 0\\ \eta_d(I(t),V(t))\cdot p(t)\cdot d & \vert\: p(t) < 0
    \end{dcases}
\end{equation}

The PI model has an internal representation of a battery's terminal voltage $V$, which is computed at every time-step $t$. The voltage of the battery is described by a function $M$, which relates the battery's energy content $b(t)$ and the applied current $I(t)$ to its voltage, as presented in equation \ref{eq:2.3}. Typically, the terminal voltage rises when the battery is charged, and drops when it is discharged relative to its open-circuit voltage in idle state.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:2.3}
   V(t) = M(b(t), I(t)) 
\end{equation}

The relation between the battery's applied power $p$, its applied current $I$, and its terminal voltage $V$ at time-step $t$ yields the internal representation of the current function over time:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:2.4}
    I(t) = \frac{p(t)}{V(t)}
\end{equation}

The BMS attached to the batteries limits the charging and discharging currents to prevent overheating and degradation of components, which is done by adding the constraint \ref{eq:2.5} to the model. The parameters $\alpha_d$ (discharging current limit) and $\alpha_c$ (charging current limit) are constants that can normally be found on a battery model's specification sheet.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:2.5}
    \alpha_d \le I(t) \le \alpha_c \\
\end{equation}

Lastly, the PI model has another constraint regarding the battery's applied current. Because a battery's voltage should not exceed certain thresholds during charging or discharging to prevent damage, and that terminal voltage rises when charged with a higher current (or falls when discharged at a higher current), the energy that can be fed to or taken from the battery varies depending on the (dis-)charging current. Therefore, an energy content range is specified, where the BMS prevents the batteries from being discharged further than a lower energy content limit $a_1(I)$, or being charged further than an upper energy content limit $a_2(I)$. These limits mark the point where the voltage reaches its allowed minimum or maximum while being (dis-)charged. The following constraint is consequently added to the PI model:

\begin{equation}
    a_1(I(t)) \le b(t) \le a_2(I(t))
\end{equation}

Given this base model, Kazhamiaka et al. describes a set of tractable implementations in their 2019 paper \cite{kazhamiaka2019tractable}. The main issue is that the three principal components (the $M$ function for computation of internal voltage, the energy content limit functions $a_1$ and $a_2$, and the inefficiency functions $\eta_c$ and $\eta_d$) have no explicit mathematical representation or are non-linear, which makes mathematical optimization difficult.

In the tractable models, these components are thus approximated with polynomials. The C/L/C model thereby uses the following approximations to retrieve a mathematical model for lithium-ion batteries:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C}onstant approximation for the battery's terminal voltage. This could be, for instance, the nominal voltage which can be found in the specifications ($V(t) = V_{nom}$).
    \item \textbf{L}inear approximation for the energy content limits depending on the current: $a_1(I(t)) = u_1I(t) + v_1$ and $a_2(I(t)) = u_2I(t) + v_2$
    \item \textbf{C}onstant approximation for the battery's charging and discharging inefficiency: $\eta_d(I(t), V(t)) = \overline{\eta_d}$ and $\eta_c(I(t), V(t)) = \overline{\eta_c}$
\end{itemize}

All in all, the C/L/C model has linear complexity while offering a decent estimation of a battery's energy level over time, but it can be seen that the discussed electrochemical reactions in the battery are at no point considered. This model is, for instance, used by the Carbon Explorer framework \cite{acun2022holistic} to represent the present energy storage of data centers.

\subsection{Single Particle Models}
\label{single_particle_models}
The Doyle-Fuller-Newman (DFN) Model, which was developed in 1993 by Doyle et al. \cite{doyle1993modeling}, is the de facto standard mathematical model for describing the electrochemical processes inside lithium-ion batteries. This model, however, is too computationally complex for many applications as it consists of coupled non-linear partial differential equations. There is often no need to spend that many computational resources on such sophisticated models when, for instance, battery packs are studied \cite{marquis2019spm, planella2021systematic}, which motivates the deployment of simpler physics-based models for analyzing larger energy storage systems.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{thesis/Images/Single_Particle_Model.pdf}
    \caption{Representation of a Single Particle Model with its submodels. The figure is adapted from Planella et al. \cite{planella2021systematic} with modifications.}
    \label{fig:single_particle_model}
\end{figure*}

Single particle models (SPMs) are considered to be the least complex electrochemical models for lithium-ion batteries \cite{marquis2019spm, ramadesigan2012modeling}, and are based on the assumption of the particles in the electrodes behaving almost the same when the battery is used at low to moderate charging rates. Thus, instead of looking at all these tiny particles separately, the model represents each electrode by a single particle to simplify the calculations, as the problem of the movement of lithium-ions in the electrodes has to be solved only for two distinct particles \cite{planella2021systematic}. Because the total electric charge entering a system must be equal to the total charge leaving the system, the current that is applied to the battery can be related to the rate in which lithium-ions are removed or inserted into the electrode particles, which means that the behavior of the entire electrode and its concentration can be calculated when the applied current is known.

Some Single Particle Models also consider the behavior of the electrolyte \cite{li2017single} in addition to the representation of the electrodes, and in similar fashion, the concentration of lithium-ions in the electrolyte can be determined. Eventually, the three described submodels can be solved using ordinary differential equations (ODEs), which are easier to handle than the coupled non-linear partial differential equations of the DFN model \cite{planella2021systematic}.  As presented in Figure \ref{fig:single_particle_model}, these determined concentrations can be used to compute battery potentials like the terminal voltage by evaluating explicit mathematical expressions with again limited computational complexity.

In conclusion, SPMs are a group of computationally inexpensive electrochemical battery models that consider physical battery traits. There also exist some open-source frameworks like, for instance, PyBaMM \cite{pybammbatteries} and liionpack \cite{tranter2022liionpack} that can be used for the simulation of batteries and battery packs that already include solvers for a Single Particle Model with and without considering the electrolyte. These will later be used for the approach evaluation in Chapter \ref{evaluation}, which is why the exact mathematical representations of the discussed principles are not mentioned at this point, as it has no relevance for this work.
